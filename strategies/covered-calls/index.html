Covered Calls: Income on Long Stock

Collect premium on stock you already own. Strike selection, expiration windows, exit rules, and how to size covered calls as part of a long-term income strategy.

Advertisement

A covered call owns 100 shares of stock and sells one call against them. The premium is income; the tradeoff is capping upside at the strike. For long-term holders who want to monetize volatility on stocks they plan to own anyway, covered calls are the simplest income strategy — no margin, no leverage, no extra risk beyond the underlying stock position.

Covered call essentials. Own 100 shares of stock. Sell one call per 100 shares. Strike 1–3% OTM for monthly income. Expiration 30–45 DTE. Take profit at 50% of premium collected. Avoid expirations through earnings.

Strike Selection

Three approaches, depending on your view of the underlying:

  • At-the-money (ATM) calls: maximum premium, immediate upside cap. Best when the stock is range-bound and you'd be willing to sell at current levels.
  • Slightly OTM (1–3% above current price): balanced approach — meaningful premium with room for upside. Default choice for monthly income.
  • Deep OTM (5%+ above): small premium, large upside room. Best when you expect the stock to move up significantly.

The standard monthly income approach: sell a call 1–3% OTM with 30–45 DTE. This collects 30–60% of the premium an ATM call would, with meaningful upside capture.

Exit Rules

Three mechanical exits for covered calls:

  • Profit target: 50% of premium collected. Close the call for half what you sold it for, capturing the bulk of the time decay.
  • Buy back if the underlying drops >10% from your cost basis. Cut losses on the underlying position and reset the call to a new strike/expiration.
  • Roll up and out if the underlying approaches the strike. Move to a higher strike in a later expiration for additional premium if you want to maintain exposure.

Avoid rolling into a worse setup. If the underlying has fallen and you want to maintain covered-call income, roll down to a lower strike (closer to current price) for additional premium. Don't roll forward into an expiration where the directional view is broken.

When Covered Calls Don't Work

Covered calls underperform in three scenarios:

  • Strong bull market: the cap on upside means you miss the big move. Premium collected is small compared to the unrealized gain you'd have on the underlying.
  • Sharp downtrend: the underlying falls, your call expires worthless (good), but you've lost money on the stock and the premium doesn't cushion the loss enough.
  • Low IV environment: call premiums are small, the income generated isn't worth the upside cap.

For directional bullish exposure without an upside cap, hold the underlying without a call. For range-bound premium collection, covered calls work. For aggressive growth, sell calls only occasionally (or not at all).

Worked Example: A 30-Delta Covered Call on 100 Shares

Suppose you own 100 shares of XYZ at $98 (acquired earlier, now trading at $100). A 30-day, 30-delta call with a $103 strike is priced at $1.40. Selling one contract collects $140 in premium against 100 shares. The annualized return on the locked-in $100 cost basis is 140 × 365/30/10000 = 17.0% if the position is rolled each cycle.

If XYZ closes above $103 at expiry, you sell your shares at $103 (the strike), your total proceeds are 103 × 100 + $140 premium = $10,440, and your realized gain is $10,440 − $9,800 = $640 (a 6.5% return on the original cost, plus premium). You miss any upside above $103 — that's the cap. If XYZ closes below $103, you keep the shares and the premium; the premium cushions a small drawdown but doesn't protect against a material selloff.

Two rules we follow: first, only run covered calls on shares you'd be comfortable owning for at least 12 months — if you'd sell at the current price, the covered call forces the discipline; if you'd hold through a 30% drawdown, the call premium is icing. Second, avoid running covered calls into a known catalyst (earnings, FDA decision, regulatory ruling) unless you're specifically trying to monetize elevated implied volatility — the call premium is rich because the market expects a big move, and the move can go either way.
