Cash-Secured Puts: Buy at a Discount, Earn Premium While You Wait

Collect premium while waiting to buy at your target price. Reserve cash sizing, strike selection, exit rules, and how to use cash-secured puts as the entry leg of the Wheel strategy.

Advertisement

A cash-secured put sells a put at a strike at or below your target buy-in price and sets aside enough cash to buy 100 shares if assigned. Premium is income; if the put expires worthless, you keep the premium and the cash. If assigned, you buy at your target — effectively at a discount equal to the premium collected.

Cash-secured put essentials. Sell a put at or below your target buy-in. Reserve 100% of strike × 100 shares in cash. Target 30–45 DTE. Take profit at 50% of premium collected. Only sell puts on stocks you'd be comfortable owning.

The 100% Cash Collateral Rule

Every short put requires cash equal to the strike × 100 shares held in reserve. A $50 strike requires $5,000 in cash; a $45 strike requires $4,500. The cash earns interest (typically in a money market fund or short-term Treasury) until the put expires or you're assigned.

The cash cannot be used for other trades while reserved. If you sell 5 puts on a $50 stock, you need $25,000 reserved. This is the binding constraint on cash-secured put sizing — there's no leverage, no margin, no naked exposure.

Standard rule: keep cash-secured put collateral under 30–50% of the trading account. The remaining cash provides diversification and a buffer for new opportunities.

Strike Selection

Strike selection depends on how aggressive you want to be about getting into the stock:

  • At your target buy-in price: maximum premium for the strike you'd be happy to own at. Default for most setups.
  • Slightly below your target: less premium but better margin of safety — only assigned if the stock falls further than you wanted to buy.
  • Below technical support: technical levels where you'd expect buying interest to step in. Reduces the probability of assignment.

The standard monthly income approach: sell a put at your target strike with 30–45 DTE. The premium should be 1–3% of the stock price (annualized, that's 12–36% if you roll monthly — though the actual realized return is lower after assignments and roll adjustments).

Exit Rules

Three exits for cash-secured puts:

  • Profit target: 50% of premium collected. Buy back the put for half what you sold it for, capturing the bulk of the time decay.
  • Hold to expiration if the put is OTM. If the put is out-of-the-money near expiration and you don't want to roll, let it expire worthless and keep the full premium.
  • Accept assignment if the put is ITM. If the underlying falls below the strike and you're assigned, you buy at the strike (effectively at a discount). Now hold the stock and consider selling a covered call (the Wheel continues).

Avoid rolling into a worse setup. If the underlying has fallen sharply and you want to maintain the position at a lower strike, roll down for additional premium. If your view has changed and you'd no longer want the stock at any price, close the put and reassess.

When Cash-Secured Puts Don't Work

Cash-secured puts underperform in two scenarios:

  • Sharp downtrends in the underlying: you get assigned at your strike, but the stock continues falling — now you're holding an unrealized loss in the underlying position. The premium collected cushions but doesn't eliminate the loss.
  • Low IV environment: put premiums are small, the income isn't worth the obligation to buy at the strike.

For both cases, the rule is the same: only sell puts on stocks you'd be comfortable owning through a drawdown. If the assignment scenario would force you to sell at a loss, don't sell the put.

Worked Example: A 30-Delta CSP on a $100 Stock

Suppose XYZ is trading at $100 and a 30-day, 30-delta put is priced at $1.20 with a strike of $93.50. To open the position, the trader reserves $9,350 in cash collateral (one round lot of 100 shares at the $93.50 strike) and collects $120 in premium (100 × $1.20). If XYZ closes above $93.50 at expiry, the put expires worthless, the trader pockets the $120, and the annualized return on the reserved cash is 120 × 365/30/9350 = 15.6% if the position is rolled each cycle.

If XYZ drops below $93.50 at expiry, the trader is assigned 100 shares at $93.50, with a cost basis of $93.50 − $1.20 = $92.30 per share. The effective entry price is meaningfully below the spot at the time of entry, which is the core appeal of the strategy: get paid to wait for a limit price. The downside is that the strategy commits cash that could otherwise earn the risk-free rate, and the put premium captures only the time value, not the protection against a gap-down.

Position-sizing rule used in our trading: never reserve more than 4% of portfolio cash for any single CSP, and never let the aggregate CSP collateral exceed 30% of the portfolio. This caps catastrophic drawdowns if a tail event (like a flash crash or earnings disaster) drives multiple assignments at once. The full position-sizing rules are in our methodology page.
