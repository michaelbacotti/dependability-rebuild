Precious Metals: Gold as a Store of Value in a New Macro Regime

Gold has had three regimes over the last 50 years: the gold-standard exit (1970s), the secular bond bull (1985–2000), and the great moderation (2001–2019).

Gold has had three regimes over the last 50 years: the gold-standard exit (1970s), the secular bond bull (1985–2000), and the great moderation (2001–2019). The 2022–2026 setup looks like a fourth regime: structurally higher real rates, persistently elevated fiscal deficits, and a multi-polar reserve dynamic. This sub-hub covers the data, the regime change, and the options positioning implications across GLD, SLV, and the mining equities (GDX).

The 50-Year Data

Gold has compounded at roughly 7.5% annually in USD terms since 1971 (the gold-standard exit). That return is comparable to long-term equity returns but with materially lower correlation — gold's correlation to S&P 500 is near zero over 50 years, slightly negative in deflationary regimes, and slightly positive in inflationary regimes. The volatility profile is much lower than equities: gold's annual standard deviation is ~15% versus ~16% for equities, but gold's drawdowns are shallower.

Implication: gold is a portfolio diversifier first, a return source second. A 5–10% allocation to gold in a multi-asset portfolio reduces total risk without materially reducing expected return.

The Central-Bank Reserve Dynamic

Central banks have been net buyers of gold since 2010, with the pace accelerating after 2022 (the Russia reserve freeze highlighted the risk of USD-denominated reserves). China, India, Turkey, and several Gulf states have been the largest buyers. The structural effect: there's a floor under gold demand from the reserve side that doesn't depend on ETF flows or jewelry demand. When ETFs are selling, central banks are typically buying — and vice versa.

The trade: monitor central-bank gold purchases via the World Gold Council quarterly demand trends report. When central-bank demand is above 200 tonnes/quarter, gold has a structural bid. Below 100 tonnes, the central-bank support weakens.

The New Macro Regime

Three structural features make the 2022–2026 regime different from the 1985–2019 secular bond bull. (1) Persistently higher fiscal deficits — the US structural deficit averaged 6%+ of GDP, supplying the market with more Treasury debt. (2) Term premium has returned to positive territory. (3) Multi-polar reserve dynamics — the USD share of global reserves has fallen from 70% (2000) to ~58% (2024), with gold and other currencies taking share.

Implication for gold: even with higher real rates (which traditionally pressure gold), the regime change is supportive. Gold trades on real rates, on USD weakness, and on macro-stress signals. All three support higher gold prices in the current regime.

Options Positioning in GLD, SLV, and GDX

Three vehicles, three vol regimes: (1) GLD — implied vol ~12–18% (similar to equities), tight bid-ask spreads, decent liquidity for covered calls and cash-secured puts. (2) SLV — implied vol ~25–35% (wider than GLD), good for calendar spreads. (3) GDX (mining equities) — implied vol ~30–45% (close to small-cap tech), good for directional plays and iron condors. The mining equities have option vol closest to tech — they're equity-driven, not gold-driven.

Specific setups: (1) Covered calls on GLD for income (low vol, high contango makes this attractive). (2) Calendar spreads on SLV capture the IV term-structure. (3) Iron condors on GDX 30–45 DTE, capturing elevated vol. Avoid long single-leg positions unless you have explicit views on the gold price.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is gold still a hedge against inflation?

Mixed evidence. Gold correlates positively with inflation in some regimes (1970s) and negatively in others (1985–2000). Over the full 50-year period, the correlation is near zero. The more reliable hedge use is against macro-stress signals — gold rallies during financial crises, geopolitical events, and reserve-currency questions. Inflation alone is not a reliable driver.

Why do central banks keep buying gold?

After the 2022 freeze of Russian central-bank reserves, several non-aligned central banks have actively diversified out of USD-denominated reserves. Gold is the largest non-USD reserve asset. Central-bank buying has averaged 1,000+ tonnes/year since 2022, well above the 200–400 tonnes/year historical pace. The structural support is real and ongoing.

What's the difference between GLD and SLV for options?

GLD tracks gold; SLV tracks silver. Silver is more volatile than gold (twice the annual standard deviation) and more cyclical to industrial demand. GLD options have lower implied vol (~12–18%) and are well-suited for income strategies (covered calls, cash-secured puts). SLV options have higher implied vol (~25–35%) and reward active vol strategies (calendars, strangles). Mining equities (GDX) have equity-level vol regimes.
