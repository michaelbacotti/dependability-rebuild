Inflation: The Measurement Problem

The headline CPI number tells you less than you think.

Advertisement

The headline CPI number tells you less than you think. Shelter and owners' equivalent rent are 32% of CPI but lag actual rents by 12 months. Trimmed Mean PCE strips out the volatile tails and is what the Fed actually watches. This hub explains why the published inflation numbers diverge from the underlying trend, and what the divergences mean for the dollar, the curve, and option pricing.

Why CPI Diverges From the Underlying Trend

The CPI is a basket of goods and services priced monthly. Most components are timely — gas prices, food, and airline fares update within days. A few — notably shelter and owners' equivalent rent — are sampled quarterly and smoothed through a model. The result is that the CPI captures the stickier, lagging components as 'inflation' while missing the real-time shifts in housing costs. Trimmed Mean PCE, the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta's preferred gauge, strips out the top and bottom 8% of monthly price changes to remove the noise. The Cleveland Fed's Median CPI does similar work. Both diverge meaningfully from the headline numbers — and that's the divergence the Fed actually responds to.

The implication for traders: a "hot" CPI print driven by volatile items (energy, food, airline fares) is not the same as a structural inflation problem. The fixed-income market often moves on the headline; the equity market should care about the trimmed mean.

The Shelter Distortion

Shelter is 32% of CPI and 15% of PCE. It's measured through a rolling six-month average of new rents and owners' equivalent rent (OER). New rents in 2024 actually fell year-over-year for most of the year, but CPI shelter remained elevated because the rolling window was still pulling in 2022–2023 highs. The lag is real and it's roughly 12 months. If new rents fall sharply, expect headline CPI shelter to follow about a year later.

For options positioning, the practical implication is that disinflation in housing is already baked into rents — it just hasn't shown up in CPI yet. The trade on falling rents is a 2025–2026 trade, not a same-quarter trade.

What the Fed Actually Watches

The Fed's preferred inflation gauge is the PCE deflator, not CPI. PCE uses a different basket (broader, includes more services) and uses chain-weighted substitution effects — when beef gets expensive, the model assumes consumers shift to chicken. CPI uses fixed weights and overstates inflation in rising categories. The trimmed-mean and median variants of both CPI and PCE aim to capture the underlying trend by removing the noise.

Watch the Atlanta Fed's sticky-price CPI (services ex-shelter, ex-medical) for the underlying trend. Watch market-based inflation expectations (TIPS breakevens, 5y5y forward) for what bond markets actually price in. The two often diverge — that's the trade.

Positioning in the Inflation Regime

Three regimes matter for positioning: (1) Falling inflation — long-dated calls benefit as the discount-rate term in option pricing compresses downward; (2) Stable inflation — IV rank tends to mean-revert, calendar spreads and iron condors work; (3) Reflation / persistent inflation — long volatility, gamma scalping, and protective puts become more attractive. The current regime (mid-2026) sits in (1) with a tail risk of (3) if oil or fiscal policy turn.

A simple framework: when 5y5y inflation breakevens are above 2.5%, expect long-dated IV to remain elevated. When they fall below 2.3%, expect IV compression across the curve. The 2.5% level is a structural edge.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does the CPI number diverge from what people experience?

Two reasons. First, the basket weights are fixed and don't update for substitution — when beef gets expensive, CPI keeps weighting beef as if people still buy it. Second, shelter is 32% of CPI but is measured through a 6-month rolling average. Actual new rents and the rent people experience diverge by 6–12 months, which is why 'inflation feels worse than it looks' in many periods.

What is Trimmed Mean PCE and why does the Fed use it?

Trimmed Mean PCE is the personal consumption expenditures price index with the top and bottom 8% of monthly price changes removed. The Fed prefers it over headline PCE because it filters out the volatile tails (energy spikes, food shocks) and captures the underlying inflation trend. It's calculated by the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas and published monthly.

When does inflation data actually matter for options pricing?

Two windows. The first is the immediate 30 minutes after a CPI or PCE print — implied vol spikes, straddle prices widen, and single-name options reprice especially in rate-sensitive sectors. The second is over the following 1–3 months — the curve steepens or flattens depending on whether the print shifts the Fed's path. Both windows are tradable, but the first is a vol event and the second is a rate-cycle event.
