Rates & Bonds: The New Regime

The bond regime changed in 2022 and is not reverting to the pre-pandemic era.

Advertisement

The bond regime changed in 2022 and is not reverting to the pre-pandemic era. Long-end Treasury yields now set the risk-asset backdrop rather than following it. The 60/40 portfolio's negative stock-bond correlation — the foundation of pension and endowment allocation since the late 1990s — is structurally weaker. This sub-hub explains what's changed, why it matters, and what it implies for options positioning.

The Term Premium Has Returned

The term premium — the extra yield long-end bonds pay over what the expected short-rate path would imply — was negative for most of the 2010s. Quantitative easing suppressed it; foreign central-bank reserve accumulation suppressed it; the secular stagnation thesis suppressed it. In 2022 the term premium turned positive and has stayed positive. The bond market is once again demanding a premium for interest-rate risk, and that premium is the dominant force shaping long-end yields.

Implications for equities: when the term premium is positive, long-bond holders demand compensation for duration risk. That compensation comes from lower bond prices — which means higher yields. Higher long-end yields force equity multiples to compress, especially for long-duration growth names. The post-2022 multiple compression in unprofitable tech is the canonical example.

The Fiscal Deficit Overhang

US fiscal deficits have averaged 6% of GDP since 2020 and are forecast to stay elevated through 2034. That supply of Treasury issuance has to be absorbed by buyers, and the marginal buyer is no longer the foreign central bank. Domestic buyers — households, pension funds, banks — require higher yields to absorb the supply. The Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee has flagged this multiple times in 2024 and 2025. The structural conclusion: the long bond is structurally heavier, with the supply-demand balance requiring higher yields for the foreseeable future.

Trade implication: short-duration over long-duration in fixed-income portfolios. In equity terms: avoid trading on the assumption that long-bond yields are mean-reverting to a 2010s-era low. The mean has shifted.

The Death of the 60/40's Negative Correlation

For most of the 1990–2020 period, stocks and bonds had a negative or near-zero correlation. A 60/40 portfolio (60% equities, 40% bonds) had portfolio-level risk lower than equities alone — the diversification was real. In 2022, when both stocks and bonds sold off simultaneously, that correlation flipped positive. It's been positive or near-zero most quarters since. The structural drivers: shared sensitivity to inflation surprises (both stocks and bonds lose when inflation surprises upward), shared sensitivity to the fiscal regime, and the term-premium mechanics described above.

Practical implication: traditional hedging via long-bond Treasuries is now less reliable. Hedge designs that worked in 2010s may need re-engineering. Long-vol overlays on equity exposure become relatively more valuable; long-bond hedges become relatively less valuable.

Options Positioning in the New Regime

Four rules: (1) Avoid short volatility on rate-sensitive names without explicit hedge for rates — vega on rate-sensitive names is partly driven by long-end yields, and the new regime makes yield-vol harder to time. (2) Calendar spreads on long-vol ETFs (UVXY, VXX) are now more attractive because the persistent term premium keeps long-end vol elevated. (3) Avoid deep out-of-the-money long puts on long-duration growth stocks as a single-leg hedge — the regime change has compressed the implied-vs-realized volatility gap. (4) Use multi-asset collars with explicit duration management for hedging.

Higher bond-equity correlation fundamentally changes how hedge overlays work. Many legacy hedges designed in the 2010s are now structurally less effective.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the term premium and why does it matter?

The term premium is the extra yield long-end bonds pay over the expected path of short-term rates. It reflects the compensation investors demand for bearing interest-rate risk. When the term premium is positive, long bonds are riskier to own — and the risk-asset backdrop gets more sensitive to long-end yield moves.

Why is the 60/40 portfolio broken?

The 60/40's diversification relied on stocks and bonds having low or negative correlation. That correlation flipped positive in 2022 and has stayed positive. A 60/40 portfolio now has portfolio-level risk closer to 70–75% equities than to the historical 50%. The diversification benefit is structurally weaker.

How do I hedge equity exposure in the new regime?

Don't rely on long-bond Treasuries as the primary hedge. Use a multi-asset collar (long equity + long put + short call), long-vol overlays via UVXY calendar spreads, or sector-specific hedges on rate-sensitive names. The hedge design has to account for the persistent positive bond-equity correlation.
