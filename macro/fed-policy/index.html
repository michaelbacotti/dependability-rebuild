Fed Policy: Kevin Warsh's New Era

The Federal Reserve under Kevin Warsh is structurally different from the pre-pandemic Fed.

Advertisement

The Federal Reserve under Kevin Warsh is structurally different from the pre-pandemic Fed. Forward guidance — the practice of telegraphing future moves to manage expectations — is gone. The reaction function is data-dependent, not formulaic. The dots in the dot plot are still published, but the market no longer reads them as binding. This sub-hub covers the regime change, what it means for the rate path, and how to position options around the new Fed.

The End of Forward Guidance

Forward guidance was the Bernanke-Yellen-Powell playbook: announce the future path, commit to it, let the market trade around the commitment. It worked when inflation was low and stable, when the Fed's word was credible, and when the economy behaved predictably. It stopped working when inflation surged in 2021–2022 and the Fed had to walk back its 'transitory' guidance. After that, the market stopped fully trusting forward guidance. Warsh's Fed acknowledged this directly: the new framework uses data-dependent, meeting-by-meeting decisions. The dots in the Summary of Economic Projections still exist but are explicitly described as 'uncertain'.

What this means for traders: stop anchoring on the Fed's prior statements. Treat each FOMC meeting as a fresh data print + a fresh forecast combination. The market's reaction function is now more volatile around Fed days than it was under forward guidance — which is tradable vol.

The Reaction Function in the New Era

Warsh's Fed watches three things above all: core services ex-shelter inflation (the sticky component), the wage growth rate, and the unemployment rate's distance from the natural rate. The Phillips Curve has weakened over the last decade — wages no longer track inflation as cleanly as they did — so the Fed now watches multiple indicators. The reaction function is also regime-dependent: in a disinflationary regime the Fed cuts quickly; in a reflationary regime it pauses; in a financial-stability regime it pivots independently of inflation.

The implication for positioning: the rate path is more sensitive to data surprises than it was under Bernanke's predictable framework. A 0.2% upside surprise on core PCE can move the dollar 1% and the curve 10 bps within hours. Sell-side rates desks now produce 'Fed Day scenario analyses' as a routine product.

Rho and Long-Dated Options

Rho — the Greek that measures option price sensitivity to interest-rate changes — matters most for long-dated options. On a 12-month LEAP, a 50 bps rate change moves the option price by approximately 2–3% (for a 0.50-delta call). On a 30-day option, the impact is barely measurable. The post-pandemic regime has higher volatility in long-end rates, so rho contribution to long-option returns is higher than it was in the 2010s.

Practical rule: if you hold LEAPS in a regime where the 10-year Treasury yield can swing 30 bps in a week, factor 5–10% of your position's mark-to-market into rho. That's not a guaranteed return — it's a sensitivity you can't hedge without a separate long-bond or futures position.

Positioning Around the New Fed

Four positioning rules: (1) Don't fade the Fed. When Warsh's Fed signals a path, treat it as the central case until the data contradict it. (2) Trade the dots-to-data gap. When the dot plot implies X and the data imply Y, the gap is a tradable vol position. (3) Don't anchor on Fed-day direction. Trade vol, not the move. (4) Use the macro regime to inform option strategy selection. Disinflationary regime favors long-dated calls; reflationary favors long volatility; stable favors calendar spreads and iron condors.

These rules are no different from what worked under Powell's Fed — but the reaction function is more sensitive, so the trades get bigger faster.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the new Fed framework under Kevin Warsh?

Data-dependence replaces forward guidance. The Fed makes meeting-by-meeting decisions based on incoming data rather than pre-communicating a path. The dot plot still exists but is explicitly described as uncertain.

How do I position options around FOMC meetings?

Trade volatility, not direction. IV typically rises into FOMC and collapses afterward — a short straddle or iron condor opened 1–2 days before and closed the day after captures the crush. For longer-dated trades, the rules are simpler: don't fight the Fed, trade the gap between dots and data.

Does the Fed really matter for options pricing?

Yes, especially through rho. Long-dated options have non-trivial rate sensitivity. A 50 bps move in long-end yields moves a 0.50-delta LEAP about 2–3%. If your portfolio holds 12-month LEAPS on rate-sensitive names (semis, biotech, utilities), the Fed's path is a meaningful part of your P&L.
